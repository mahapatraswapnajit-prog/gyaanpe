Class 6 Science Chapter 4: Exploring Magnets

NCERT Curiosity · Simple notes, key terms and practice questions

Magnets attract iron, nickel and cobalt. Each magnet has a north and south pole; like poles repel and unlike poles attract. A freely suspended magnet points north–south, which is how a compass works. Magnets can be made by rubbing iron with a magnet and should be stored carefully to keep their strength.

Key concepts

🧲Magnetic Materials

Magnets attract materials like iron, nickel and cobalt. These are magnetic materials.

Example: An iron nail sticks to a magnet; a plastic spoon does not.

In real life: Fridge magnets stick to steel doors.

💡 Did you know? Not all metals are magnetic: copper and aluminium are not.

🔴Poles

Every magnet has two poles, north and south, where attraction is strongest.

Example: Iron filings gather mostly near the ends of a bar magnet.

In real life: Magnets are tested by where pins cling.

💡 Did you know? Cutting a magnet gives two smaller magnets, each with two poles.

🔄Attraction and Repulsion

Like poles repel; unlike poles attract.

Example: N–N push apart; N–S pull together.

In real life: Maglev trains float using magnetic repulsion.

💡 Did you know? Repulsion is the surest test of a magnet.

🧭Direction and Compass

A freely suspended magnet rests in the north–south direction. A compass uses this to show directions.

Example: Sailors used compasses to navigate.

In real life: Hikers use compasses in forests.

💡 Did you know? Natural magnets are rocks called lodestone (magnetite).

🛠️Making and Keeping Magnets

An iron piece can become a magnet by rubbing it with a magnet in one direction. Heating, dropping or hammering can weaken magnets.

Example: Store bar magnets in pairs with unlike poles together and keepers across ends.

In real life: Electromagnets lift scrap iron.

💡 Did you know? Keep magnets away from phones and cards.
