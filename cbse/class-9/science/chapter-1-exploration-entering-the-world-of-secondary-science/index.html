Class 9 Science Chapter 1: Exploration: Entering the World of Secondary Science

NCERT Exploration · Simple notes, key terms and practice questions

The chapter introduces secondary science: the scientific method, SI units, accuracy and precision, variables in experiments, models and lab safety.

Key concepts

🔍How Science Works

Science moves through observation, questioning, hypothesis, experiment, analysis and conclusion. A result that does not fit leads to a better question.

Example: Noticing that iron gates rust faster near the sea, then testing salt water vs plain water.

In real life: Medicines are approved only after careful trials.

💡 Did you know? Scientific ideas change when new evidence appears.

📏SI Units

Scientists use the International System (SI): metre (length), kilogram (mass), second (time), kelvin (temperature), ampere (current), mole (amount) and candela (light).

Example: Speed is measured in metres per second (m/s).

In real life: A common system lets scientists across countries share results.

💡 Did you know? There are 7 SI base units; all other units are built from them.

🎯Accuracy and Precision

Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value; precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other.

Example: Readings 5.1, 5.1, 5.2 cm are precise; if the real length is 5.1 cm they are accurate too.

In real life: Pharmacists weigh medicines precisely.

💡 Did you know? Taking an average of repeated readings reduces random error.

🧪Variables

The independent variable is what you change, the dependent variable is what you measure, and controlled variables stay the same.

Example: Changing water temperature to see how fast sugar dissolves.

In real life: Farmers compare fertilised and unfertilised plots.

💡 Did you know? A control set-up gives a baseline for comparison.

🦺Models and Safety

Scientists use models — diagrams, equations, simulations — to explain things too big, small or fast to see. Lab safety rules protect everyone.

Example: A globe is a model of the Earth.

In real life: Weather forecasts run on computer models.

💡 Did you know? Every model has limits; it is never the real thing.
